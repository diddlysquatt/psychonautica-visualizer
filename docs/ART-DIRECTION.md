# Art direction: what makes a scene belong here

After reading this you can judge your own scene the way a reviewer will, before anyone else sees it.
This is the taste of the project's founder, Stoned Ape, gathered from many rounds of watching every scene with
music. The quoted lines are his words.

## Who it is for

> "I think of these like a person who's tripping: something somebody would just get completely lost in while
> listening to music. They aren't going to want to fine-tune everything themselves. They just want to press
> play and put on the music."

The Auto Show is the product. Judge a scene by what a person sees over minutes of it with music on, hands off,
not by one beautiful still.

## What a scene should have

**Enormity.** "Always try to go for enormity of size with these scenes, and have the details really reflect
all of it." The feeling of "this is enormous, this is huge". Pull the camera back and the world keeps going:
a city to the horizon, a wall of a thousand books, one endless flower. A small object floating in black is
the opposite. The camera travels through the levels of detail, out to the enormity and in to the fine work,
and never sits close for long ("like a dude shaking his butt in front of you at a concert" was the verdict on
one scene that did).

**Saturated colour with ink between it.** Deep colour that stays colour, and dark between the colours so
they read: at least a quarter of the frame dark is a good rule. Loud passages are the test.

**Its own material.** "We don't have to always have it be that psychedelic, all the bright neon colored
stuff. I want to have so much variety that you never get bored with one kind of style throughout, and
everything has its own feel." Fire-lit stone, stained glass, tapestry, engraved metal, paper, deep jewel
tones are as welcome as neon. Choose one and commit.

**Designed ornament, everywhere, at every scale.** Detail that looks drawn on purpose: mouldings, filigree,
characters, lettering, pattern with structure. Detail that pays off when the quality is turned up. Nothing
that looks like clip art or a placeholder beside the rich parts; no part of the frame that is merely filled.

**Like dancing.**

> "The movements of everything always be very fluid and never predictable, but always going with the music,
> never too jerky, having that rhythm to it. It's like dancing. You don't ever want to be too rigid, and you
> want to be loose while also keeping right in on the beat."

Many distinct ways to move, one per kind of sound and per kind of musical event, combined differently
through a visit so that two songs never look alike. Bass, mids and highs each visibly drive their own thing.
Punches and steps on the beat, loose in between.

**Transformations that land on the drop.**

> "Not scene transitions, but a transition within that very scene too, where it happens right at the best
> drops, and it goes, bam, and it changes. ... It makes people think, how the hell is it doing that? How did
> it just know?"

A change of formation, structure, palette region, camera mode or material. Not a flash. It lands exactly on
the arrival (`F.onImpact`) or the new section (`F.onSection`), and a build winds up to it (`F.tension`).

**Variety and a little weirdness.** "Variety is the spice of life." A scene should keep surprising after
many viewings: seeds that give clearly different variants, states it only reaches sometimes. Humour is
welcome. So is strangeness.

## What gets a scene sent back

In rough order of how often it happens:

1. **Washout.** Pale haze, glow drowning the detail, colours summing toward white, "trying too much with the
   colors". The number one complaint across the whole project. Judge at the default settings in loud
   passages.
2. **One hue flooding the frame.** A whole picture of orange, or teal, edge to edge.
3. **Dark passages and black frames.** Including the moment between two states of a transformation, the
   first seconds of a visit, silence, and music without a beat.
4. **Texture.** Anything that reads as speckle, noise or wallpaper at a glance, especially zoomed out.
   If the thumbnail does not read as a place or a thing, it is texture.
5. **Sway.** Slow, wavy, aimless motion in place of punches and steps. Also its opposite: hyperactive,
   chaotic motion, and anything that jerks.
6. **Strobing and flicker.** Whole-frame flashes on every kick or hat. Light may snap; the whole picture
   may not.
7. **Too close for too long.** A camera that sits inside the scene so that its structure cannot be seen.
8. **Giant plain shapes and masks.** A big flat disc or bar across the frame, cut-off shapes at the edges
   of a tile, anything that breaks the illusion of one continuous world.
9. **Cheap parts beside rich ones.** A low-detail filler, a flat sprite in a 3D world, "filler objects
   bolted onto a scene". Extend the scene's own structure instead.
10. **Gimmicks.** A move that says "look at this" instead of belonging to the world: a slideshow wipe, a
    camera trick with no reason.

## How to judge your own sheet

- Look at the contact sheet at thumbnail size first, then at full size. Thumbnail: does each frame read as
  a scene? Is every rung of the zoom ladder full? Full size: is the detail crisp and designed?
- Compare loud frames with quiet ones. Colour must stay colour when the music is loud.
- Check the three hardest views: fully zoomed out, a fold, and the frame halfway through your transformation.
- Play three kinds of music: something with a four-on-the-floor kick, something with no drums, something
  dense and fast. The scene should look made for each.
- Be your own harshest critic. A reviewer will look at the same sheet.

## The signature

Many built-in scenes hide the words STONED APE somewhere in their world, small and in the scene's own
style, sometimes with a mushroom. They are intentional. A contributed scene is welcome to hide its own
maker's mark in the same spirit: readable when found, never obvious, a few percent of the frame at most.
